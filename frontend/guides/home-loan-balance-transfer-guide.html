Home Loan Balance Transfer: When It's Worth It (and When It's Not)

A balance transfer (BT) moves your outstanding home loan to a new bank, usually to grab a lower interest rate. It can save real money — but it isn't automatically worth it just because the new rate looks smaller. Here's how to actually work out whether it makes sense for you.

What a balance transfer actually is

The new bank pays off your outstanding balance with your current lender, and you start a fresh loan with the new bank — same remaining principal, new interest rate, and usually a choice to keep the same remaining tenure or adjust it. It's not a top-up by default (though most banks will offer you additional funds on top of the transferred amount, called a "balance transfer + top-up" — treat that extra borrowing as a separate decision from the transfer itself).

The good news: if your existing loan is floating-rate (true for most home loans), RBI rules mean your current bank can't charge you anything to close it early — the only real costs are on the new bank's side. That materially improves the math in favour of transferring when a genuinely better rate is available.

The breakeven math

A lower rate only helps once it's paid for its own switching costs. The quick way to check:

Breakeven (months) ≈ Total switching costs ÷ Monthly interest saved

If the breakeven period is short compared to your remaining tenure, transferring is likely worth it. If you're planning to prepay/foreclose the loan soon anyway, or your remaining tenure is short, a transfer may never pay for itself — you'd pay the switching costs but not have enough remaining months to recoup them.

Worked example

Suppose you have a ₹40 lakh outstanding balance, 15 years remaining, currently at 9.5% with Bank A. Bank B offers 8.25% on a balance transfer, with ₹20,000 in total switching costs (processing + legal/technical, no foreclosure charge since it's floating-rate).

  • EMI at Bank A (9.5%, 15 yrs): ₹41,769/month
  • EMI at Bank B (8.25%, 15 yrs): ₹38,806/month
  • Monthly saving: ₹2,963
  • Breakeven: ₹20,000 ÷ ₹2,963 ≈ under 7 months

With 15 years (180 months) remaining and a breakeven of just 7 months, this transfer is almost certainly worth it — you'd be saving money for the remaining 173+ months. The same 1.25-point rate gap on a loan with only 18 months left would breakeven much later relative to the time remaining, making it a much closer call.

Other things to check before transferring

  • Fixed vs floating: if your current loan is fixed-rate, factor in the old bank's foreclosure charge (up to 2%) — this can erase much of the benefit of a small rate gap.
  • Reset clauses: some "special" transfer rates are teaser rates that reset higher after a year or two — read the fine print, not just the advertised rate.
  • Tenure reset: if the new bank resets your tenure back to a longer period to lower the EMI, you may pay more total interest even at a lower rate. Compare total interest, not just the monthly EMI.
  • Credit score impact: a balance transfer involves a fresh loan application and a credit check, which can cause a small, temporary dip in your credit score.
  • Existing relationship benefits: some borrowers get rate discounts, fee waivers or faster service from their current bank for other products (salary account, credit card) that a switch could affect — factor in the whole relationship, not just the loan.

A simple rule of thumb

Balance transfer tends to make sense when: the rate gap is at least 0.5–0.75 percentage points, your remaining tenure is long (well beyond the breakeven period), and you're on a floating-rate loan (so the old bank can't charge a foreclosure penalty). The smaller the rate gap, the shorter the remaining tenure, or the higher the switching fees, the less likely it is to be worth the hassle.
