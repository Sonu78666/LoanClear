Home Loan Prepayment Guide: How to Save Interest the Right Way

Prepaying a home loan — putting extra money toward the principal beyond your regular EMI — is one of the simplest ways to cut the total interest you pay. This guide covers how it works in India, what RBI actually allows, and which prepayment strategy saves the most.

What is loan prepayment?

Every EMI you pay is split into two parts: interest (on the outstanding balance) and principal. Early in the loan, most of your EMI goes toward interest. A prepayment is any extra amount paid beyond the scheduled EMI, applied directly to reduce the outstanding principal. Because interest is calculated on the outstanding balance each month, a smaller balance means less interest for every month that follows — which is why prepaying early in the loan saves far more than prepaying late.

Foreclosure (or full prepayment) is the same idea taken to its conclusion — paying off the entire remaining balance in one go and closing the loan before its scheduled tenure ends.

Is there a prepayment penalty in India?

This is where most borrowers get confused. The rule, per the Reserve Bank of India:

  • Floating-rate loans to individual borrowers (which covers almost all home loans) — no prepayment or foreclosure penalty, regardless of the source of funds.
  • Fixed-rate loans — lenders may charge a prepayment penalty, typically up to 2% of the prepaid amount.

If you're on a floating-rate home loan, there is no financial downside to prepaying whenever you have spare cash — the only real decision is whether prepaying beats other uses of that money (see the section below on prepay vs. invest).

How EMI and prepayment interact

The standard EMI formula is:

EMI = P × r × (1+r)n / ((1+r)n − 1)

where P is the outstanding principal, r is the monthly interest rate (annual rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100), and n is the remaining number of monthly installments. A prepayment reduces P immediately — which either shortens n (loan closes earlier, EMI unchanged) or reduces future EMI (tenure unchanged, EMI drops), depending on what you ask your lender to do with the recalculated schedule. Most borrowers choose to keep the EMI the same and shorten the tenure, since that saves more total interest.

Worked example

Take a ₹45 lakh floating-rate home loan at 7.5% for 20 years (EMI ≈ ₹36,252). Left untouched, you'd pay roughly ₹42.0 lakh in total interest over 20 years. Now add a ₹3 lakh prepayment every year:

  • Total interest drops to roughly ₹16.6 lakh — a saving of about ₹25.4 lakh
  • The loan closes in about 8 years 8 months instead of 20 years — more than 11 years earlier

These numbers depend heavily on your own rate, tenure and how early you start prepaying — the earlier in the loan you prepay, the more interest you save, because you're cutting interest off more remaining months.

Should you prepay, or invest the money instead?

Prepayment is effectively a guaranteed, risk-free "return" equal to your loan's interest rate — paying off a 7.5% loan early is like earning a guaranteed 7.5% on that money, tax considerations aside. If an alternative investment (equity, debt funds, etc.) can reliably beat your loan's interest rate after tax, and you're comfortable with that investment's risk, investing may come out ahead over the long run. If you'd rather have certainty and lower EMI stress, prepayment wins. Many borrowers do both — investing through market dips and prepaying during years when markets look expensive.

A note on tax benefits

Prepaying reduces your outstanding principal, which reduces the interest portion of your remaining EMIs — and therefore your Section 24(b) interest deduction (capped at ₹2 lakh/year for a self-occupied property) shrinks over time as there's simply less interest to deduct. This is a minor offsetting factor, not a reason to avoid prepayment — the interest you save is still far larger than the tax deduction you give up.
