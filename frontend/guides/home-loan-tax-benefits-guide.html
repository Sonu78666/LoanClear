Home Loan Tax Benefits: Section 80C, 24(b) & 80EEA Explained

A home loan isn't just a way to buy a house — it's also one of the few remaining large tax deductions available to salaried and self-employed borrowers in India. Here's exactly which sections apply, how much you can actually claim, and the one catch that trips up most people: it depends on which tax regime you pick.

The two main deductions

SectionWhat it coversAnnual limit
80CPrincipal repayment₹1.5 lakh (shared with PF, ELSS, life insurance, etc.)
24(b)Interest paid₹2 lakh (self-occupied); no cap if let-out, but see below

Section 80C: principal repayment

The principal portion of your EMI qualifies for deduction under Section 80C, up to ₹1.5 lakh a year. The catch: this limit is shared with every other 80C investment you make — EPF/VPF contributions, ELSS mutual funds, PPF, life insurance premiums, children's tuition fees, and more. If you're already maxing out 80C through EPF and other investments, your home loan principal may add little to no extra benefit here.

One more condition: if you sell the property within 5 years of possession, any 80C deduction you've already claimed on the principal gets added back to your taxable income in the year of sale.

Section 24(b): interest paid

Interest is usually the bigger number, especially in the early years of a loan, and it's deducted separately from principal under Section 24(b):

  • Self-occupied property — up to ₹2 lakh/year in interest deduction.
  • Let-out (rented) property — no cap on the interest deduction itself, but the overall loss from house property (rental income minus interest, when interest exceeds rent) that can be set off against your other income (salary, etc.) is capped at ₹2 lakh/year. Any excess loss carries forward for up to 8 years, to be set off against future house-property income.

Unlike 80C, pre-construction interest (paid before possession) isn't lost — it can be claimed in 5 equal instalments starting the year construction/possession is complete, still within the same 24(b) limits.

Section 80EEA: extra interest deduction (limited eligibility)

80EEA offered an additional ₹1.5 lakh interest deduction (on top of 24(b)) for first-time buyers of affordable housing — but only for loans sanctioned between 1 April 2019 and 31 March 2022, with the property's stamp duty value capped at ₹45 lakh. That sanction window has closed, so it no longer applies to new loans — it's only relevant if you already have a loan sanctioned in that period and are still claiming it. Check with a tax advisor if you're unsure whether your loan qualifies.

The one thing that trips people up: the tax regime.
Since the new tax regime became the default (Budget 2023), Section 80C and 24(b) deductions on a self-occupied home loan are only available if you opt for the old tax regime. The new regime's lower slab rates come with most deductions removed. If a home loan is a major part of your tax planning, run the numbers both ways before choosing a regime each year — the deduction isn't automatic anymore. (Interest on a let-out property remains deductible against rental income under both regimes, since it's a cost of earning that income, not a standalone deduction.)

Joint home loans: double the benefit

If a home loan is taken jointly — say with a spouse — and both are co-owners of the property and co-borrowers on the loan, each person can separately claim up to ₹1.5 lakh under 80C and ₹2 lakh under 24(b), in proportion to their share of the EMI. For a couple splitting repayment 50/50, that's a combined potential deduction of up to ₹3 lakh (80C) + ₹4 lakh (24(b)) across both incomes — a significant reason many couples choose joint home loans even when one income alone could service the EMI.

Worked example

Take a ₹45 lakh self-occupied home loan at 7.5% for 20 years (EMI ≈ ₹36,252). In the first year, roughly ₹3.3 lakh of the EMIs paid is interest and about ₹1 lakh is principal.

  • 80C claim: ₹1 lakh (full principal paid, under the ₹1.5 lakh cap)
  • 24(b) claim: ₹2 lakh (capped, even though actual interest paid is higher)
  • Under the old regime in the 30% tax slab, that's roughly ₹90,000 in tax saved in that year alone (30% of ₹3 lakh combined deduction, approximately — exact figures depend on your full income and other deductions)

As the loan matures, the interest component of each EMI shrinks and the principal component grows — so the 24(b) claim tends to fall below its cap in later years while the 80C claim can rise, within its own cap.

A note on prepayment and tax benefits

Prepaying your loan reduces the outstanding principal, which shrinks the interest portion of future EMIs — and therefore your 24(b) deduction shrinks too, since there's simply less interest to deduct. This is a minor offsetting factor, not a reason to avoid prepayment: the interest you actually save by prepaying is almost always far larger than the tax deduction you give up.
