Home Loan Tax Benefits: Section 80C, 24(b) & 80EEA Explained

A home loan isn't just a way to buy a house — it's also one of the few remaining large tax deductions available to salaried and self-employed borrowers in India. Here's exactly which sections apply, how much you can actually claim, and the one catch that trips up most people: it depends on which tax regime you pick.

The two main deductions

SectionWhat it coversAnnual limit
80CPrincipal repayment₹1.5 lakh (shared with PF, ELSS, life insurance, etc.)
24(b)Interest paid₹2 lakh (self-occupied); no cap if let-out, but see below
80EEAExtra interest, first-time affordable-housing buyers₹1.5 lakh, on top of 24(b) — only for loans sanctioned 1 Apr 2019–31 Mar 2022

Section 80C: principal repayment

The principal portion of your EMI qualifies for deduction under Section 80C, up to ₹1.5 lakh a year. The catch: this limit is shared with every other 80C investment you make — EPF/VPF contributions, ELSS mutual funds, PPF, life insurance premiums, children's tuition fees, and more. If you're already maxing out 80C through EPF and other investments, your home loan principal may add little to no extra benefit here.

One more condition: if you sell the property within 5 years of possession, any 80C deduction you've already claimed on the principal gets added back to your taxable income in the year of sale.

Section 24(b): interest paid

Interest is usually the bigger number, especially in the early years of a loan, and it's deducted separately from principal under Section 24(b):

  • Self-occupied property — up to ₹2 lakh/year in interest deduction.
  • Let-out (rented) property — no cap on the interest deduction itself, but the overall loss from house property (rental income minus interest, when interest exceeds rent) that can be set off against your other income (salary, etc.) is capped at ₹2 lakh/year. Any excess loss carries forward for up to 8 years, to be set off against future house-property income.

Unlike 80C, pre-construction interest (paid before possession) isn't lost — it can be claimed in 5 equal instalments starting the year construction/possession is complete, still within the same 24(b) limits.

Section 80EEA: extra interest deduction (limited eligibility)

80EEA offered an additional ₹1.5 lakh interest deduction (on top of 24(b)) for first-time buyers of affordable housing — but only for loans sanctioned between 1 April 2019 and 31 March 2022, with the property's stamp duty value capped at ₹45 lakh. That sanction window has closed, so it no longer applies to new loans — it's only relevant if you already have a loan sanctioned in that period and are still claiming it. Even within that window, it required you to be a genuine first-time homebuyer (no other residential property owned on the date of sanction) plus the other conditions above — it was never automatic just because a loan fell inside the date range. Check with a tax advisor if you're unsure whether your loan qualifies.

Old regime vs new regime: what actually survives

This is the single biggest source of confusion. Since the new tax regime became the default (Budget 2023), most home-loan deductions are only available under the old regime — but the rule is slightly different for a self-occupied property versus a rented-out one.

BenefitOld regimeNew regime
80C — principal repaymentAvailable, subject to conditions & the shared ₹1.5L limitNot available
24(b) — self-occupied interestAvailable, up to ₹2L/yearNot available
24(b) — let-out property interestAvailable; loss set-off against other income capped at ₹2L/yearInterest is still considered while computing house-property income, but the resulting loss cannot be set off against salary/other income heads

In short: for a self-occupied home, choosing the new regime means giving up both 80C and 24(b) entirely. For a let-out property, interest still reduces your rental income for tax purposes under either regime, but only the old regime lets a resulting loss offset your salary or other income. If a home loan is a major part of your tax planning, run the numbers under both regimes before choosing each year — the deduction isn't automatic. (Source: Income Tax Department's guidance for salaried individuals; tax rules change, so confirm the current-year position with a tax advisor.)

Joint home loans: double the benefit

If a home loan is taken jointly — say with a spouse — and both are co-owners of the property and co-borrowers on the loan, each person can separately claim up to ₹1.5 lakh under 80C and ₹2 lakh under 24(b), in proportion to their share of the EMI. For a couple splitting repayment 50/50, that's a combined potential deduction of up to ₹3 lakh (80C) + ₹4 lakh (24(b)) across both incomes — a significant reason many couples choose joint home loans even when one income alone could service the EMI.

These are potential combined limits, not guaranteed deductions. Each co-borrower must independently satisfy the applicable eligibility conditions — co-ownership, an actual (not just nominal) share of repayment, the property's use, and choosing the old tax regime — and can only claim in proportion to their real ownership and repayment share, not an equal split by default.

Worked example

Take a ₹45 lakh self-occupied home loan at 7.5% for 20 years (EMI ≈ ₹36,252). In the first year, roughly ₹3.3 lakh of the EMIs paid is interest and about ₹1 lakh is principal. This example assumes:

  • The borrower has opted for the old tax regime (required for either deduction below)
  • They fall in the 30% marginal tax slab
  • The full ₹1 lakh principal repaid is an eligible 80C investment (i.e. they haven't already used up the ₹1.5 lakh shared limit elsewhere)
  • The full ₹2 lakh self-occupied interest cap under 24(b) is available to them
  • 80C claim: ₹1 lakh (full principal paid, under the ₹1.5 lakh cap)
  • 24(b) claim: ₹2 lakh (capped, even though actual interest paid is higher)
  • At a 30% marginal rate, 30% of the ₹3 lakh combined deduction works out to roughly ₹90,000

That ₹90,000 is a purely illustrative calculation under the assumptions above — not a guaranteed saving. Your actual benefit depends on your real tax slab, how much of your 80C limit is already used elsewhere, other deductions and exemptions you claim, and applicable cess/surcharge. Two borrowers with the identical loan can end up with very different actual tax savings.

As the loan matures, the interest component of each EMI shrinks and the principal component grows — so the 24(b) claim tends to fall below its cap in later years while the 80C claim can rise, within its own cap.

A note on prepayment and tax benefits

Prepaying your loan reduces the outstanding principal, which shrinks the interest portion of future EMIs — and therefore your 24(b) deduction shrinks too, since there's simply less interest to deduct. This is a minor offsetting factor, not a reason to avoid prepayment: the interest you actually save by prepaying is almost always far larger than the tax deduction you give up.
